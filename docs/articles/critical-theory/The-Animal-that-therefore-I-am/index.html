“The Animal that therefore I am” (2006)

Jacques Derrida

“The Animal that therefore I am” is probably the most important philosophical work to consider the relation between humans and animals. It is a fiery and radical text which exposes and overturns the entire western philosophical tradition by calling for an urgent change in the ways that nonhuman animals are treated.

The title

The title of Derrida’s work plays on one of the best known phrases in philosophy. Rene Descartes (1596 – 1650) famously proves that he exists with the phrase “cogito ergo sum,” “I think therefore I am.” This deeply humanist statement connects human existence with the ability to think. Derrida’s disruptive title reminds us of something that we all know, but that we pretend not to: that we are animals.

Nudity

Derrida begins his text with an anecdote. He recounts feeling ashamed when standing naked in front of his cat. This example opens the discussion of what it is that distinguishes humans from animals. Derrida argues that the humanist tradition is founded on the idea that humans and humans only have the ability to be naked. Nudity is a function of the removal of clothing, and since nonhumans do not wear clothes, they cannot be naked. To be clear, Derrida is deeply suspicious of this distinction. He believes that making an absolute distinction between humans and animals is a fundamentally violent gesture. He uses the concept of nudity to explore and explode this distinction.

The bible narrates the origin of man in relation to nudity. According to Genesis, the fall of man happens when humans eat from the tree of knowledge. Simultaneously, they gain knowledge of good and evil and realise that they are naked. Hence nudity is connected with the very origin of man according to the Judeo-Christian tradition. More than this, we see that what makes man man is his knowledge and his morality – being able to distinguish between good and evil. This is the moment of the separation of humans from nonhumans. The qualities afforded to humans are denied to nonhumans. If humans have knowledge, animals do not. If humans are moral creatures, nonhumans are not. If humans are naked, nonhumans are not.

Derrida calls this “the awful tale of Genesis.” He goes on to note how the book opens a systematic violence against nonhumans which starts at the very beginning with the naming of the animals. He understands this act, as one of subjection whereby nonhumans are tamed and domesticated through the act of naming. He notes that it is man alone, before the creation of woman, who names animals, hence the act connects to masculine domination.

This moment also reveals another layer of meaning in Derrida’s title. The original French reads L’Animal que donc je suis. Here Derrida is punning on the final word since suis sounds the same as suivre which means “follow.” The title, therefore, also means “the animal that therefore I follow.” Derrida uses this word to describe the relationship between humans and nonhumans. Humans follow nonhumans inasmuch as nonhumans came first, both in the biblical tale and in evolution. But Derrida also uses the term to describe the ways in which humans are after nonhumans, out to get them, to hunt them, to track them down, to cause them harm. Here again, he is stressing the deeply unethical nature of the relation between humans and nonhumans. Nonhumans were here first, but humans, the latecomers, have exerted their control and dominance.

Violence and suffering

Derrida hypothesises that human-animal relations have undergone a fundamental change which dates back around 200 years. This date points to the onset of industrialisation which overhauled the ways in which nonhumans were treated, notably in the meat industry. Derrida is indicating that the use of various technologies have fundamentally changed the way that nonhumans are understood. He is talking about industrialised farming methods, mass experimentation, breeding, genetic manipulation, cloning and pharmaceutical interventions.

Derrida deplores the morality of human-animal relations in the strongest terms. No-one, he says, can deny that human-animal relations are violent. No-one can deny the suffering that nonhumans experience at the hands of humans. With this gesture, Derrida dismisses the Cartesian philosophical tradition which denies that non-humans can suffer. But going further, Derrida makes a direct comparison between the meat industry and the holocaust. If anything, however, the meat industry is worse since it is

As if, for example, instead of throwing a people into ovens and gas chambers (let’s say Nazi) doctors and geneticists had decided to organize the overproduction and overgeneration of Jews, gypsies, and homosexuals by means of artificial insemination, so that, being continually more numerous and better fed, they could be destined in always increasing numbers for the same hell, that of the imposition of genetic experimentation, or by gas or by fire.

In other words, the meat industry is worse than the holocaust since it not only kills on an industrial scale, but breeds at the same time, producing slaughter and suffering without end on an ever greater scale.

Drawing on the writing of British philosopher Jeremy Bentham, Derrida makes strategic use of the idea of suffering which he uses to articulate the moral necessity for concern about the meat industry. He describes suffering as a “nonpower at the heart of power.” Here, Derrida is noting that the humanist tradition has always made demands of nonhumans when asking whether moral consideration should extend to them. Can they think? Are they conscious? Can they make tools? Etc. The difference with the question of suffering is that it is not a positive ability, rather it is characterised by passivity. You don’t need any special abilities in order to suffer. In this way, Derrida neatly sidesteps the question of animal abilities and whips the rug from beneath the feet of philosophers seeking to justify the violent treatment of nonhumans.

The animot

As ever, Derrida is alert to the power of words. The naming of the animals discussed above is a speech act, where Adam uses language to bring about a new state of affairs in the world. Derrida sees this act as a kind of original sin as it is instrumental in subjecting animals to the cruel power of humans. At an even more fundamental level, Derrida recognises that the word “animal” is perhaps at the root of the violence done to nonhumans.
